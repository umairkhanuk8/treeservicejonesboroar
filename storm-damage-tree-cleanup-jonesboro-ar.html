Storm Damage Tree Cleanup for Jonesboro and Craighead County Properties

After a severe thunderstorm, straight-line wind event, or ice accumulation passes through Jonesboro, properties are often left with a mix of damage types—broken limbs hanging in canopies, fallen trees across driveways or fence lines, split trunks still standing but structurally compromised, scattered debris across lawns and landscape areas, and trees that look intact from the street but have sustained internal damage that isn't immediately visible.

Cleaning up after a storm involves more than hauling wood. The sequence matters: identify immediate hazards first, address trees and limbs still in contact with structures, clear blocked access, then move into the broader debris cleanup and assessment phase. Rushing through cleanup without assessing remaining trees can create follow-up hazards that are just as serious as what was addressed immediately after the storm.

Post-Storm Tree Assessment Matters

Trees that appear intact after a storm may have sustained root plate disturbance, internal splitting, or hanging wood lodged in the upper canopy. A post-storm walkthrough of the property identifies these concerns so they can be addressed before the next weather event. We include this assessment as part of the cleanup scope when requested.

What Storm Damage Tree Cleanup Covers

Storm cleanup work typically begins with removing the most obvious hazards—fallen trees blocking access routes, limbs resting on rooftops or structures, and hanging wood in the canopy over occupied or high-use areas. Once those priorities are handled, the work shifts to the broader cleanup of broken limbs, uprooted smaller trees, and storm debris across the property.

  • Fallen tree removal from driveways, yards, fences, and access routes
  • Controlled removal of trees or limbs resting against structures
  • Hazardous limb removal — hanging and partially attached branches
  • Split trunk assessment and removal where structural failure has occurred
  • Storm debris cleanup — brush, limbs, and scattered wood
  • Chipping and hauling of debris based on property owner preference
  • Post-storm assessment of remaining standing trees

Sequencing the Cleanup Work

Good storm cleanup follows a logical sequence. After life-safety concerns are addressed—making sure no one is near energized lines or unstable structures—the crew assesses the property systematically. Trees in contact with structures are the first work priority because their position creates ongoing load on the building. Once those are handled, blocked access routes are cleared. Then the broader debris cleanup begins.

For properties where multiple trees have fallen or where debris is spread across a large area, we organize the work so equipment can move efficiently across the site. Brush goes through the chipper while log sections are cut and staged for loading. Lawn and landscape areas are protected during equipment movement where possible. Older central Jonesboro neighborhoods with established mature trees and close-set structures require more careful equipment planning than open suburban lots.

Assessing Trees That Are Still Standing

Some of the most significant storm damage isn't immediately visible from the ground. Limbs lodged in the upper canopy—what arborists sometimes call "widow makers"—can remain suspended for days or weeks before shifting and dropping. Root plate disturbance from saturated soil conditions may cause a tree to develop a new lean that wasn't there before the storm. Split wood in the upper crown may not be apparent until the canopy dries and the affected wood becomes visible.

When we assess your property after a storm, we look at both the fallen material and the trees still standing. Any tree with visible canopy damage, a new lean, root plate movement, or a split in a major stem that could affect neighboring structures is noted and discussed with you. Whether those trees require immediate attention—through hazardous tree removal, limb removal, or monitoring—depends on what the assessment shows.

Salvageable vs. Non-Salvageable Trees

Not every storm-damaged tree needs to be removed. Trees with broken limbs that can be pruned cleanly, with trunks that remained intact, and with root systems that were not significantly disturbed may continue to grow well after appropriate cleanup pruning. Trees that lost a portion of their crown but retained the main structure and a significant percentage of live wood may recover with proper care.

Trees where the trunk split deeply, where root plate failure is evident, where the majority of the crown is lost, or where the remaining structure poses an ongoing risk to structures or frequently used areas are more likely candidates for removal. We evaluate each tree individually and share our observations honestly. We don't assume every storm-damaged tree needs to come out, and we don't recommend preservation when the structural reality doesn't support it.

Insurance Documentation and Property Coordination

For property owners coordinating with homeowners insurance or commercial property insurance after storm damage, we can provide documentation of the work completed and the conditions observed on-site, when requested and authorized. Insurance decisions rest with your insurer and your policy—we cannot determine what coverage applies to your situation, and we don't make coverage guarantees. What we can do is perform thorough work and document it accurately if that information is needed for your claim process.

For commercial properties, apartment communities, and HOA-managed sites, we coordinate cleanup scope and scheduling with property managers or building owners. For larger properties with significant storm damage across multiple areas, work may be phased across more than one visit. Our Jonesboro storm-damage tree team handles both the immediate response and the follow-up cleanup as a coordinated project when that's what the property needs.

Stump Grinding and Site Restoration

After storm-damaged trees are removed, stumps can be ground as part of the same project or scheduled as a follow-up. For properties where a tree fell and the root plate pulled out of the ground, the area left behind may need soil work in addition to stump and root grinding. We discuss the full restoration scope during the assessment so you understand what's involved in getting the area back to usable condition.

Our professional tree cleanup for local properties covers the complete scope of post-storm work—not just felling the tree and leaving. The property should be in better condition when we leave than when we arrived, and that means handling the debris, cleaning the site, and confirming no additional hazards remain before the crew departs.

Common Questions

Frequently Asked Questions About Storm Damage Tree Cleanup in Jonesboro

How soon after a storm can cleanup work begin?
▼

Work can begin once the site conditions are safe to access—active weather has passed, any utility hazards have been confirmed clear, and the work area can be safely established. After major storm events when many properties are affected simultaneously, response is prioritized based on hazard level. Properties with active structural damage or blocked access are addressed before general debris cleanup requests. Call us to describe your situation and we'll give you an honest assessment of response timing.

What if some of the storm-damaged trees are still standing but look damaged?
▼

Trees that survived the storm but show visible crown damage, new lean, root plate disturbance, or split wood in the upper canopy should be assessed before being assumed safe. Post-storm damage is not always immediately apparent, and a tree with lodged debris or internal splitting may present a follow-up hazard in the next weather event. We include standing tree assessment as part of our storm cleanup scope when requested, and we'll share observations about any trees that warrant attention.

Can you work around the structure if a tree is resting on a roof?
▼

Yes, but it requires careful planning. Trees resting on structures are under load, and removing them without accounting for the tension can cause them to shift and cause additional damage. We assess the contact points, the load distribution, and the rigging options before making any cuts. The work involves relief cuts to reduce tension, rigging to control the direction of movement as sections are freed, and coordination around the structure to protect it during removal. We do not rush this type of work.

Does storm damage cleanup include debris hauling?
▼

Brush and smaller limbs are chipped and removed. Log sections are cut and loaded for hauling. Some property owners want to keep wood for firewood—we're happy to cut and stack sections if that's the preference. The extent of debris hauling included in the job is confirmed during the assessment and reflected in the estimate so there are no surprises about what gets removed versus what stays on-site.
